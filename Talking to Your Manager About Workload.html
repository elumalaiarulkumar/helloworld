A short, practical guide

Talking to your manager when the workload gets heavy

The goal of this conversation isn't to vent — it's to give your manager something they can actually act on. These five moves will help you get there.

1

Get specific before you talk

Vague stress is hard to act on. Specific facts are easy to act on.

  • List what's actually on your plate right now, with rough time estimates.
  • Note what's slipping or will slip, and by when.
  • Decide what you need: fewer things, more time, more help, or clearer priorities. Pick one or two, not everything at once.
2

Open with the situation, not the emotion

Lead with facts. Let the manager draw the conclusion that you're stretched, rather than opening with "I'm stressed."

"I want to flag my workload before it affects delivery. Right now I'm carrying X, Y, and Z, and I think one of them is going to slip this week."
"Can we find 20 minutes to look at my plate together? I think some of this needs to move or get reassigned."
3

Name the stakes, plainly

It's fine to say the human part too — just pair it with impact, so it reads as information, not a complaint.

"I've been working late most nights to keep up, and it's not sustainable at this pace. I want to fix this before quality or my health takes the hit."
4

Bring options, not just a problem

Managers can act faster on a choice than on an open-ended problem.

  • "I can push back the deadline on A, or hand off B to someone else — which makes more sense on your end?"
  • "If nothing can move, I need help deciding what 'good enough' looks like on the lower-priority items."
  • "Could we agree on what I should drop if something new comes in this week?"
5

Close with a concrete next step

End the conversation with something that will actually change, not just a sense that you were heard.

"So to confirm — I'll deprioritize A, you'll let the team know, and we'll check in again Friday to see how it's landing. Does that work?"

A few things to avoid

✕

Apologizing repeatedly for raising it. You're giving useful information, not confessing a failure.

✕

Comparing your load to a coworker's. It shifts the conversation away from what you actually need.

✕

Waiting until you're at the breaking point. Raise it a notch earlier than feels urgent — it reads as foresight, not alarm.

✕

Leaving without a next step. "Thanks for listening" isn't the same as "here's what changes."

If workload stress has been going on for a while and isn't easing after conversations like this, it's worth also talking to a doctor or a counselor — this guide covers the workplace conversation, not the toll it can take on you.
